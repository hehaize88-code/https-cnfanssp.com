## Establish what the photographs actually show
Start a Hacoo QC photo check by identifying the source of the images. A catalogue photograph, a creator's post, another customer's photograph and an image of your own received item do not provide the same evidence. Ask which item, variant and date the images refer to. A polished gallery can show the intended design while telling you little about the condition of an individual unit. Never label a promotional picture as proof that your order has passed quality control.

This walkthrough applies whenever relevant inspection images are available, including photographs you take after delivery. It does not assume that every Hacoo order includes warehouse photographs or a pre-shipment approval stage. Use only the features actually available for your order. If the evidence is a public photo from another buyer, treat it as a source of questions for the listing rather than a substitute for checking the item you receive.
## Check identity before judging workmanship
Place the expected option beside the image record: product type, colour, selected size, version, quantity and included components. Compare those facts before zooming into stitching. A beautifully finished item in the wrong size is still the wrong candidate for the order. A picture of a complete bundle does not prove that every component belongs to your selected option. Confirm that the label or reference in the photo can be connected to the item being discussed.

For a clothing example, compare the design and option label with the saved listing. For a bag, confirm the shape and the strap or accessories included in the chosen bundle. For shoes, establish that the two shoes are the same selected size and colourway when the available evidence allows it. If identity is uncertain, request clarification first. Detailed visual scoring cannot repair a missing connection between the photographs and the actual order.
## Request a useful set of angles
Begin with a full view that shows the item without important areas cut off. Then inspect the back, sides, interior or underside where those views matter to the product. A close-up can reveal a seam but hide the overall alignment; a wide image can show shape but conceal a small tear. Use both scales. Ask for the one additional angle that answers the unresolved question instead of requesting more photographs with no purpose.

For instance, if a bag's closure is hidden, a view of the closure and its attachment is more useful than another front photograph. If the hem appears uneven, a straight-on image with the garment laid flat is more informative than a tilted handheld shot. Keep requests descriptive and observable. Avoid asking whether a product is perfect or authentic when the answer would require evidence beyond a photograph.
## Inspect visible construction without predicting durability
Look at seams, edge finishing, fasteners, attachment points and areas where material joins. Check for visible gaps, loose parts, tears, stains or inconsistent alignment. Compare symmetrical areas under a similar angle. Record the observation precisely: a seam appears open at a particular location, or a fastener looks misaligned. Distinguish that observation from a conclusion about why it happened or how long the product will last.

Photographs cannot establish every aspect of material quality, comfort, strength or long-term performance. A dark line may be a shadow, and a loose thread may need another view before its significance is clear. Request a closer photograph when the answer changes your decision. Do not turn a visual checklist into an authenticity certificate or a numerical quality rating unsupported by physical inspection. A useful QC note states what is visible and what remains untested.
## Use measurement photos with clear endpoints
A ruler in a picture is useful only when its position answers the measurement question. Confirm the starting point, ending point, units and whether the item is flat or stretched. A tape held at an angle can mislead. For garment width, check that the requested width is measured between the same endpoints used in the listing. For a bag, distinguish external dimensions from usable internal space. For shoes, do not treat outsole length as internal length.

Compare the image with the exact size chart and selected option. If a label says one size but the measured dimension appears different, preserve both pieces of evidence and ask for clarification. Do not automatically select whichever number fits your expectation. Any stated tolerance should come from the listing or a relevant instruction, not from a universal rule invented by the reviewer. A measurement check should reduce uncertainty rather than disguise it behind a tape measure.
## Treat colour and texture cautiously
Lighting, camera processing and screens can change the appearance of a colour. Compare images taken under reasonably similar conditions and ask for a neutral-light view if a colour difference is important. A single strongly coloured background can also affect perception. Describe the visible difference without claiming that a screen image proves the exact shade of the physical item.

Texture is similarly limited. A photograph may show a surface pattern, but it cannot reliably establish softness, fabric weight, fibre composition or how a material feels in use. Read the relevant listing information and distinguish listed claims from independently observed facts. If a material property is essential to your purchase, unresolved evidence may be a reason to pause. Repeating that the photograph looks premium adds confidence without adding information.
## Build evidence for a specific problem after delivery
If an item arrives damaged, wrong or incomplete, keep the packaging long enough to document the issue. Capture the outer parcel, shipping label, product bag, full item and the affected area where relevant. For a size discrepancy, include the size label and a clear measurement. For missing components, show the received contents and the order's stated bundle. Save originals privately; redact addresses, phone numbers and barcodes before sharing anything publicly.

Hacoo's return guidance distinguishes different problem types and may require different evidence. Check the current order instructions and deadline promptly. Do not wash, alter or discard relevant material before understanding the process. Do not send a return to an address found in an old article; follow the instructions for your case. A clear evidence sequence helps explain the problem but does not guarantee eligibility, reimbursement or a particular support decision.
## End with an action, not a vague verdict
For each important check, write one of three outcomes: supported by the available image, needs another view, or visible mismatch. Add the exact observation and the next action. For example, a size label may match while chest width remains unknown; request the specific width instead of declaring the whole garment approved. If the main issue is visible damage, preserve the evidence and use the responsible service's after-sales process.

Keep the final conclusion proportional to the evidence. Photos can identify a wrong option or show visible defects; they cannot prove authenticity, guarantee comfort or predict every hidden problem. A good inspection record is short enough to use and specific enough to act on. It connects the ordered option, the relevant image, the unresolved fact and the next step. That is more valuable than a confident pass based only on attractive catalogue photography.

## Turn one unclear image into a focused request
Imagine a hypothetical bag photograph in which the front panel is clear but the strap attachment is hidden behind the body. The useful note is not that the bag looks good overall. It is that the attachment cannot be assessed from the supplied angle. Request a view showing the attachment, its surrounding material and enough of the bag to preserve context. Once that image arrives, record only what it actually establishes. A visible attachment can confirm that the part is present and show obvious damage; it does not measure load capacity or prove long-term durability. This example applies the same sequence used throughout the guide: identify the exact uncertainty, request evidence that addresses it, then keep the conclusion within the limits of that evidence. Avoid replacing a missing observation with reassurance.
