## Start by identifying what the screenshot shows
To find a Hacoo product from a screenshot, first identify the object you want rather than searching every visible detail at once. Is the screenshot from a product page, a shopping basket, a creator’s outfit post or a collage? A product-page screenshot may include a useful item reference or selected colour. An outfit post may show only a styling suggestion, with no evidence that the exact item is currently available through Hacoo.

Save the original screenshot and note the source and date if you know them. Crop a separate copy around the relevant object while preserving the original context. Remove faces, account details, addresses and order numbers before uploading an image to any third-party search service. This site’s spreadsheet search accepts text, not image uploads. The workflow below helps you turn visible details into a better text query, then evaluate the results without mistaking resemblance for a verified match.
## Extract distinctive clues before adding brand names
Write down the product category, dominant colour, cut or shape, material appearance and two unusual details. For a hoodie, useful clues might be a zip front, a large back graphic, a contrasting hood lining or a particular pocket arrangement. For a bag, note the handle shape, strap attachment, closure and compartment layout. For shoes, look at the sole profile, panel arrangement and lacing rather than relying only on colour.

Treat material appearance as a visual clue, not a confirmed composition. A smooth surface does not prove leather, and a fuzzy image does not establish wool content. A visible logo can help identify a design reference, but it does not prove authenticity or the identity of the seller. Begin with the features you can actually see. If the screenshot is too small to read text reliably, record the text as unclear instead of building the entire search around a guessed word or product code.
## Build short searches and change one clue at a time
Start with a compact query that combines the category and the most distinctive feature, such as “grey zip hoodie back graphic”. If the results are too broad, add a visible shape or colour detail. If they disappear entirely, remove the least certain term. Change one clue at a time so you can tell whether a particular word improved the results. Copying a long social caption often introduces unrelated styling terms and makes the search less useful.

Use the spreadsheet’s relevant category when you know the product type, then compare a small set of candidates. Catalogue titles may use different languages or abbreviated descriptions, so try a common synonym where appropriate: “trainers” and “sneakers”, or “shoulder bag” and “crossbody bag” when the visible construction supports both. These are search experiments, not claims that every term has high search volume. Do not keep adding brand names to force results that do not share the screenshot’s visible features.
## Use image search as a candidate finder
If you choose a separate image-search service, upload the cropped object image after reviewing its privacy controls. An image search can reveal visually similar pages or earlier uses of a photograph. It may help you recover a product name or find a clearer reference image. It does not establish that a particular shop has stock, that the listing is genuine, or that the item delivered will match the picture.

Compare the original screenshot with each candidate rather than accepting the first visually similar result. Reused supplier images can appear on several listings with different variants, dimensions or materials. A match to the photograph is therefore only one clue. Save the candidate URL and note what agrees and what remains unknown. If a search service returns a marketplace or catalogue page, continue to the actual listing and check its selected option instead of treating the search thumbnail as the final answer.
## Verify the candidate on the live product page
Open the candidate page and compare three distinctive details from the screenshot. Check both the front and back when available, plus any detail that would be difficult to explain as a lighting difference: pocket placement, seam layout, handle construction or the sole pattern. Then inspect the selectable colour and size. A listing containing many styles can show the right item in one image while defaulting to a different variant in the selector.

Record the title, URL, product reference and selected option together. Recheck the current price and availability on the destination page; an old screenshot or our historical catalogue card is not a live offer. If the destination changes to an unrelated item, do not treat the old link as a verified match. Use the saved details to search again. Also distinguish the external catalogue linked by this site from the Hacoo app: a catalogue result is not proof that the same product is available in your Hacoo account or country.
## Compare three candidates using visible evidence
Imagine a screenshot of a black shoulder bag with a curved top, one short handle and a metal buckle. Candidate A matches the colour but has a straight top. Candidate B matches the silhouette and buckle but lacks a clear handle photograph. Candidate C matches all three visible details, while its dimensions are missing. This hypothetical example shows why “looks similar” is too vague to make the final choice.

Keep a short record for each candidate: matching details, conflicting details, unknown facts and the next check. You might discard A, request a clearer handle view for B and check measurements for C. Do not fill missing dimensions with estimates from the photograph unless you explicitly label them as uncertain; perspective can distort the apparent scale. Limiting the shortlist to three candidates makes it easier to compare meaningful differences instead of collecting dozens of near-identical links that you never verify.
## Handle unavailable links and country differences
A screenshot can outlive the original listing. The item may be removed, the link may point to a different variant, or availability may depend on the account or destination. Check whether the page is genuinely unavailable or simply requires the normal app or sign-in flow. Use the product reference and visible details to search again if the original listing cannot be recovered. Keep the previous URL as context rather than repeatedly sharing it as a working purchase link.

Do not change your delivery country to an address you cannot use merely to reveal a product. Confirm availability for the destination you actually intend to enter at checkout. If a result remains inaccessible, record the limitation and move to another candidate. The links-not-working guide covers this situation in more detail. A successful search means finding a usable, checked listing for your circumstances, not simply making a thumbnail appear in a search-results grid.
## Move from a visual match to an informed decision
Once you have a plausible match, check size information, selected quantity, shipping cost and return conditions before deciding to proceed. A screenshot cannot answer those questions. Use the size guide for measurement comparisons, the sneaker QC guide for photo and insole checks, or the general QC checklist for other visible details. Keep any remaining uncertainty explicit, particularly when the only evidence is a supplier photograph shared across multiple listings.

Finish by saving a small reference pack: original screenshot, checked candidate URL, selected variant, date and unresolved questions. If you later receive an item that differs from the selected description, those records can help explain the discrepancy through the order-support process. They do not guarantee a refund or an authenticity conclusion.
